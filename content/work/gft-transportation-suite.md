## The problem

Engineering firms are full of good tools nobody else can find. A spreadsheet that checks bolt spacing, a macro that post-processes an analysis model, a script that fills in wind loads: each one saves hours for the person who wrote it and for almost nobody else. They live on personal drives, they are not reviewed, and when their author changes projects they quietly stop being used.

The cost is easy to miss because nothing breaks. The same problem is simply solved again, in another office, by someone who never knew the first solution existed.

## The product

GFT Transportation is a centralized website for the firm's transportation automation tools, developed together with IT. I serve as its product owner and architect, and I hold the same two roles for the GFTSteelBridgePal application that sits within it.

The bridge tools that feed it come from real project work:

- OpenBridge to CSiBridge model generation ([Steel Bridge Pal](/work/steel-bridge-pal))
- Automated input of user-defined wind loads in CSiBridge
- STAAD.Pro post-processing and automated connection design
- Shop-drawing review tools for repetitive geometric checks
- Engineering review utilities

The history of most of these is in [A decade of engineering automation](/work/engineering-automation).

## My role

**Product owner.** I set the priorities for what gets built, based on where project teams lose the most time. As Automation & AI Ambassador for Northeast Transportation I collect use cases from staff, and those feed the backlog.

**Architect.** I define how a tool written by one engineer becomes something the whole practice can rely on: a common home, standardized inputs and units, and validation an engineer can see.

**Translator.** I work between practicing engineers and IT. I can describe an engineering workflow precisely enough to build, and explain a software constraint in terms an engineer accepts.

## How tools earn their place

The approach is project-driven. A tool goes through three steps:

1. **It starts on a real job.** Someone has a deadline and a repetitive task, and writes something to get through it.
2. **It solves a real problem.** The tool earns its keep on that project before anyone talks about reuse.
3. **It is standardized for reuse.** Inputs, units and checks are brought to a common pattern so a second team can use it without the author in the room.

I coach colleagues on taking their own tools through the third step, and I presented the approach to the Bridge Practice Design and Resources Subcommittee.

One rule does not bend: automation and AI assist the engineer, and a person verifies the result. That principle carries into the firm's AI-assisted plan-review and inspection-report QA/QC work, which I contribute to as a member of the AI Working Group.

## What it shows

Building one good tool is engineering. Getting many tools adopted across offices is product work: prioritizing, standardizing, training and earning trust. This role is where I do that.
